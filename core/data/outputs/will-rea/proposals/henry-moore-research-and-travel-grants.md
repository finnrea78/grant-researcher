## 1. Project Framing

Rea is one of a small number of UK-based art historians with sustained, fieldwork-grounded expertise in Yoruba and Ekiti sculpture — a tradition that sits squarely within the Henry Moore Foundation's broad definition of sculpture history. The critical asset here is continuity: Rea held a £105,000 Henry Moore Foundation Research Grant (2006–09), and this application should be positioned explicitly as the next phase of a research programme the Foundation already knows and has invested in. The scheme's purpose — enabling travel and archival work for individual sculpture historians — maps precisely onto what Rea needs to complete two manuscripts in preparation. The application must be framed as scholarly research into the history of African sculpture, making clear from the first sentence that Rea is an academic researcher, not a practising artist (the scheme excludes funding that develops an artist's own practice).

## 2. Research Question Suggestions

Each option proposes a discrete, short trip with a defined deliverable proportionate to a £2,500 budget.

### Option A: Ekiti fieldwork — sculptors and patrons for the monograph

Travel to Ekiti (Ikole Ekiti, Ado Ekiti, and surrounding communities) to conduct field interviews with sculptors, carvers, patrons, and custodians of Epa masquerade materials, in direct support of the submitted chapter in Picton (ed.), *William Fagg and African Art* and the wider manuscript on sculptors of the Ekiti tradition. Rea has thirty years of fieldwork access in Ikole Ekiti; this visit would update the longitudinal record, photograph extant sculptures, and gather oral histories from ageing practitioners. Deliverable: a completed manuscript section or journal article drawing directly on the new field materials. This is the strongest option thematically — it ties the HMF's founding interests to the field's foremost specialist's ongoing programme.

### Option B: UK archival research — British Museum and Horniman collections

A focussed visit to the British Museum Department of Africa, Oceania and the Americas and the Horniman Museum to examine unpublished archival material relating to Ekiti sculptors and Epa masquerade objects — including accession records, correspondence, and field photographs acquired during the colonial period and by mid-twentieth century collectors (including William Fagg, whose archive relates directly to Rea's submitted chapter). This option links naturally to Rea's existing HMI consultancy on the display of British Museum masks. Deliverable: archival notes and reproductions feeding into the Picton volume chapter and the masquerade monograph. This is the lowest-cost option — London travel and accommodation are well within £2,500.

### Option C: Nigeria — Lagos archives and the John Randle Museum project

Travel to Lagos to conduct archival research at Lagos State heritage collections and the John Randle Museum of Yoruba Cultural Heritage (for which Rea is project curator and academic lead), with extension to Ibadan and Ife. Deliverable: archival and photographic documentation feeding into both the museum interpretive programme and the Cambridge UP *Africa displaying / Displaying Africa* manuscript. Strong impact narrative (live national-scale museum project), but requires supplementary funding — Lagos return flights alone typically exceed £700–900.

## 4. Methodology Notes

The scheme is designed for exactly this type of activity: archival research and fieldwork travel for sculpture history. Rea's method combines archival research (colonial-era records, accession documents, held in UK collections) with ethnographic fieldwork (structured interviews, object documentation). The application should name specific archives to be visited, specific questions that cannot be answered from Leeds, and the specific manuscript sections that will benefit. Reviewers want proportionality — a targeted trip with a defined output, not an open-ended expedition.
